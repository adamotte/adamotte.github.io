The token paradox

August 2026 · 10 min read · Anthony Damotte

Token prices are falling tenfold every year, and yet enterprise AI bills have never climbed faster. This is no accounting anomaly: it is Jevons paradox at work, and it changes the very nature of the AI budget — which stops being an overhead line item and becomes an architecture parameter.

Discuss on LinkedIn
In brief
  • Deflation without industrial precedent: ÷10 per year at constant quality — GPT-3-level quality went from $60 to $0.06 per million tokens in three years.
  • And yet enterprise AI spend was × 3.2 in one year ($11.5B → $37B); $644B of worldwide GenAI spend in 2025 (+76%). Jevons had it all figured out in 1865.
  • The cause: agents — 4× more tokens than chat, 15× for multi-agent, up to 1000× for agentic coding. Since February 2026, machines have been consuming more tokens than humans.
  • The shift: token volume explains 80% of the performance variance of an agentic system. You don't cut tokens, you place them — an architect's decision now, no longer a procurement negotiation.
01 · The founding anecdote

€500 in a single afternoon

At my first agentic AI hackathon, I burned through five hundred euros' worth of tokens in a single afternoon — half the budget set aside for eighty participants. At the time, the figure drew smiles, and it still comes up in conversation now and then. But with hindsight, the anecdote tells a different story than mere engineering gluttony: it foreshadowed, in miniature, the economic transformation every finance department is discovering today. An agent that actually works consumes on a scale that bears no comparison with a human having a conversation. And that is precisely why it pays off.

02 · The deflation

Raw material in free fall

Let us begin with the cheerful side. At equal performance, the price of inference is collapsing at a pace no other technology has ever matched — not even the semiconductor in the glory years of Moore's law.

Epoch AI's caveat

The fastest deflation rates come from the most recent year observed — their persistence is not guaranteed. This paper's thesis does not depend on it: it rests on the differential between deflation and consumption, not on its exact value.

03 · The explosion

And yet, the bills keep exploding

If the unit price is divided by ten every year, budgets should be melting away. The opposite is happening, with remarkable violence.

$644B

04 · Jevons, verified in 13 months

From prophecy to measurement

A nineteenth-century economist had already understood this mechanism completely. William Stanley Jevons observed in 1865 that the more coal-efficient the steam engine became, the more coal England burned: efficiency does not reduce the consumption of a resource, it multiplies it by opening up uses that were previously out of reach.

  • January 2025 — when DeepSeek sends markets into a panic by proving that models can be served for a fraction of the accepted cost, Satya Nadella answers with a single sentence: “Jevons paradox strikes again! As AI gets more efficient and accessible, we will see its use skyrocket”[7].
  • ~February 1, 2026 — on OpenRouter, the largest independent multi-model aggregator, tokens consumed by agentic systems overtake those consumed by humans — a crossover established on 450 trillion tokens of real traffic; agentic traffic weighs ~15× more tokens per request[8].

Thirteen months between the prophecy and its measurable verification. Deflation did not shrink the bill: it made the agent economically viable, and the agent blew up the volume.

05 · The token factory

What an agent actually consumes

To understand why, you have to look at what an agent does with its context.

Agent~4×Anthropic, measured in production[9]
Multi-agent~15×Anthropic[9]
Agentic codingup to ~1000×Stanford Digital Economy Lab[10]

The cost is dominated by input tokens: at every iteration of its loop, the agent re-reads its entire context, which snowballs[10]. The same study reveals a more unsettling fact: on an identical task, the execution cost of the same agent varies by a factor of thirty from one attempt to the next, and agents are incapable of predicting their own consumption. We have industrialized a workforce whose wages are unpredictable to within 3,000%.

But there is a counterpart, and it is decisive: at Anthropic, token volume alone explains 80% of the performance variance of the system[9]. Tokens are not a cost leaking away; they are literally the material the quality of the result is made of.

06 · The tipping point

Budget becomes an architecture parameter

If token volume determines performance, then deciding how much an agentic system is allowed to consume, and where, amounts to drawing its architecture. Each of these questions is simultaneously a question of quality and a question of cost:

  • How many adversarial checks per deliverable?
  • How much loop depth before escalating to a human?
  • Which model for which tier — an expensive frontier model for the final arbitration, a compact model for bulk triage?
  • Should the whole context be re-read, or is it worth investing in a memory that summarizes it?

Prompt caching illustrates the shift: properly architected, re-reading the context costs ten times less[11] — but that is a design decision, not a procurement negotiation.

Organizations have caught on at spectacular speed: the FinOps Foundation measured 31% of teams managing AI spend in 2024, 63% in 2025 — and 98% in 2026, with dedicated indicators such as cost per inference[12]. Gartner notes AI budgets coming “under increased scrutiny, with an emphasis on usage efficiency, cost control and measurable outcomes”[13]. And yet, in the largest survey of teams running agents in production, cost ranks only behind quality on the list of blockers[14] — proof that practitioners have internalized the lesson: you don't cut tokens, you place them.

07 · Takeaways

Three convictions

Budget for production capacity, not a software license.

The right indicator is not the monthly bill, it is the cost per verified outcome — and you must accept that more ambitious outcomes mean higher consumption.

The token budget belongs in architecture documents.

On the same footing as latency or availability. An agentic system that cannot say where its tokens go is a system that cannot be steered.

Beware of easy savings.

Deflation is working for you: what you refuse to consume today will cost ten times less next year. But the competitors who learned a year earlier how to place their tokens will not be caught up at a discount.

As for my five hundred hackathon euros: set against what they taught me about harness design — lessons I have been applying to production systems ever since — they are probably the cheapest training I have ever received. The token paradox fits in one sentence: yesterday's waste becomes tomorrow's under-investment, and the only real mistake is to treat as an accountant what has become an architect's decision.
