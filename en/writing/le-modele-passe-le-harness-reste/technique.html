Models come and go, the harness remains

August 2026, updated September 2026 · 11 min read · Anthony Damotte

Five frontier models shipped in the first three weeks of September 2026, and each of them will be pulled from the API that serves it within a year or two. Building an AI strategy on a model is building on sand. While models depreciate, evals, the harness, the company's memory and its data gain value. That is why an agentic factory exists.

Discuss on LinkedIn
Key takeaways
  • A frontier model stays in the API for about a year before it is retired (5 to 20 months depending on the case). Providers document this themselves, and releases are speeding up: five frontier models in three weeks in September 2026.
  • AI pilot failures (about 95% with no P&L effect according to MIT, over 40% of agentic projects headed for cancellation according to Gartner) come down to organization more than technology. In McKinsey's survey, the share of companies seeing an effect on earnings did not move between 2025 and 2026, while models changed several times.
  • The gap between capability and reliability will not close by waiting for the next model: two years of capability gains brought only modest reliability gains (Princeton). The harness is what closes it.
  • The durable assets are evals, context and memory, standardized integration and guardrails. They have to be yours: OpenAI shuts down its hosted evals platform on November 30, 2026.
01 · The wrong question

“Which model should we choose?”

It is the question I hear most often in workshops. It resembles the structuring choices of an earlier era, the database, the language or the cloud, which were settled for a decade. A model, though, is not chosen like a foundation. It wears out, it gets replaced, and its lifespan is counted in months. September 2026 offered an almost caricatural illustration[1].

Notice periods range from sixty days at Anthropic to roughly six months at OpenAI. Anthropic now lists a minimum retirement date for each active model, set one year after release. In late 2025 it also made formal commitments to preserve the weights of retired models, which amounts to admitting that forced migrations are structural and that they cost money[7]: a central component of our systems now changes about once a year.

03 · The misunderstanding

Rereading the “95% failure rate”

According to MIT's NANDA project, roughly 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots had no measurable effect on the P&L[8]. The figure made the front page of Fortune and briefly rattled the markets[9].

An honest reading of the number

Critics rightly noted that any pilot not rolled out at scale counts as a failure, and that the report mostly describes organizations poorly equipped to evaluate, buy and integrate[10]. That corrected reading is the interesting one, and other studies back it up.

Between the two McKinsey surveys, models were replaced several times over and improved considerably. The effect on the bottom line did not move: the difference lies around the model, in the organization and in the tooling.

04 · The gap

Capability and reliability do not progress together

A model's raw capability and a system's reliability are two separate quantities:

  • τ-bench (Sierra), now a reference for customer service agents: the best agents succeeded on fewer than half of the tasks, and their success rate fell to around 25% when eight consecutive successes on the same task were required (pass^8)[14]. An agent that succeeds once will not necessarily succeed eight times in a row.
  • Princeton (Arvind Narayanan's team, ICML 2026) gave the gap a name, the “capability-reliability gap”. Across twelve reliability metrics applied to fifteen models, nearly two years of spectacular capability gains produced only modest reliability gains[15].

Waiting for the next model will not close the gap. Rules, checks, guardrails and error recovery narrow it. That is the harness, in the literal sense: the gear that lets you hitch a powerful but unpredictable force and turn it into useful work.

05 · The durable assets

What gains value as the model loses it

Evaluations, the first asset

“Your eval is the most valuable AI asset you own, not your model, not your prompts.” Drew Breunig[16], following on from Greg Brockman's “evals are surprisingly often all you need”, written as OpenAI's president[17].

An evaluation suite that captures what your business calls good work puts you in charge of the calendar: every new model becomes a candidate you can audition in a few hours, and a forced migration becomes routine. Hamel Husain observes that failing AI products nearly all share the same root cause, the lack of a solid evaluation system[18]. Berkeley's work on “criteria drift” shows that evaluation criteria are discovered through use, by reading what the system produces[19]. Every organization therefore forges its own, and nobody can sell them off the shelf.

Evals that belong to you

On June 3, 2026, OpenAI announced the closure of its hosted evals platform, its Agent Builder and its reusable prompts API. Evals become read-only on October 31 and everything shuts down on November 30; the company recommends moving to Promptfoo, an open-source tool that runs locally or in continuous integration[20]. A vendor's tooling lives on the vendor's schedule, and your test sets have to be able to do without it.

Context and memory

Andrej Karpathy popularized “context engineering”, which he describes as “the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step”[21]. Anthropic turned it into an engineering discipline of its own, one that treats context as “a finite resource with diminishing marginal returns”[22]. What that context holds is mostly what the company knows: its business rules, its conventions, its living documentation, the history of its decisions. Structured to reach agents at the right moment, that memory serves today's model and its successors.

Integration, now standardized

The Model Context Protocol, launched by Anthropic in late 2024 and adopted by OpenAI, Google and Microsoft, was handed in December 2025 to a neutral foundation under the Linux Foundation, with more than ten thousand public servers at the time[23]. The LLMOps tooling market is growing by more than 20% a year[24]. Connectors outlive the models that pass through them.

The harness, something you can measure

  • SWE-bench Mobile (production iOS codebase, 22 agent configurations): with the same model, a factor of six separates the best harness from the worst[25].
  • Meta-Harness (Stanford): a coding agent inspects and rewrites the harness itself, and beats the state of the art in context management by 7.7 points with four times fewer tokens[26].
06 · The manifesto

Four agentic factory rules

Never tie yourself to a model.

Every architecture should pass the replacement test: if the provider retires your model on Monday, switching takes a few days, with evals to back it up. If it takes a month or more, the architecture has a problem.

Budget evals like infrastructure.

Sheltered from schedule trade-offs. It is the only AI investment whose value rises mechanically over time, since every failure met in production feeds the test suite.

Treat memory and context as intellectual property.

What your agents know about your company belongs to you: versioned, audited, portable from one model and one provider to the next, evals and prompts included.

Industrialize the loop, beyond the prompt alone.

Guardrails, cross-checks, error recovery: the loop is what turns an impressive demo into a system you dare to depend on.

If the model is a consumable and the harness an asset, the split of investment should flip: less energy spent chasing this week's leaderboard, more spent building what lets you get the most out of any model. Models will come and go, that is what they do, and nobody knows which one will win the next round. When it arrives, what will count is already having in hand the one thing it cannot bring you: the machine for turning it into value.
