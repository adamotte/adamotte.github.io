Models come and go, the harness remains

August 2026 · 11 min read · Anthony Damotte

A frontier model now lives twelve to eighteen months before it is retired from the API that serves it. Building your AI strategy on a model is therefore building on sand — and yet that is exactly what most failing projects do. What accumulates and appreciates while models depreciate goes by other names: evals, harnesses, memory, data. That is where the investment belongs, and it is the entire reason an agentic factory exists.

In every technology leadership conversation there is one question I am invariably asked, often within the first few minutes: “which model should we choose?” I understand the question — it resembles the structuring choices of an earlier era: the database, the language, the cloud. But it rests on a false premise: the idea that a model is chosen the way one chooses a foundation, for the long term. An AI model is not a foundation. It is a high-performance consumable, with a lifespan measured in months. The real question — the one that separates the 5% of projects that create value from the rest — is: what are you building around it that will outlive the model?

Twelve to eighteen months

Model churn is not an impression; it is public data anyone can verify on the providers' deprecation pages. At Anthropic, Claude Sonnet 3.7, launched in February 2025, was retired twelve months later to the day; Opus 4 and Sonnet 4 will have lived thirteen months; Opus 4.1, twelve[1]. At OpenAI, GPT-4.5-preview lasted less than five months between launch and retirement[2]. The contractual notice periods give the measure of the phenomenon: sixty days of guaranteed minimum at one, six months for stabilized models at the other. In late 2025, Anthropic even published formal commitments on preserving model weights, acknowledging in black and white what every production team already knows: forced migrations are structural, costly, and here to stay[3]. This is the actual regime we build in: a central component of our systems gets replaced, willingly or not, on an annual cadence.

The 95% misunderstanding

It is against this backdrop that the most talked-about statistic of 2025 deserves a second reading: according to MIT's NANDA project, roughly 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact[4]. The figure made the front page of Fortune and briefly rattled the markets[5]. It deserves its caveats — critics rightly pointed out that any pilot not deployed at scale is counted as a failure, and that the report documents less a failure of the technology than an organizational inability to evaluate, buy, and integrate[6]. But it is precisely this corrected reading that interests me, because it is corroborated on every side. Gartner predicts that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, owing to escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls[7]. McKinsey finds that 88% of organizations use AI somewhere, that only 39% derive enterprise-level financial impact from it, and that the factor most correlated with value is neither the model nor the budget but the redesign of workflows — which only 21% of adopters have undertaken[8]. One telling detail from the MIT report: solutions built with a specialized partner succeed twice as often as improvised internal builds[4]. Everywhere, the same conclusion in negative space: the model was never the problem. What is missing is the factory around it.

The gap between capability and reliability

Why does the factory matter more than the machine? Because a model's raw capability and a system's reliability are two quantities that do not progress together. Sierra's τ-bench, now a standard for customer-service agents, showed it first: the best agents completed fewer than one task in two, and above all their success rate collapsed when asked to succeed at the same task eight times in a row — only about 25%[9]. A Princeton team led by Arvind Narayanan has just formalized this “capability-reliability gap”: across twelve reliability metrics applied to fifteen models, nearly two years of spectacular capability progress produced only modest reliability gains[10]. In other words, waiting for the next model will not close the gap. What closes it is everything you build around it: the rules, the checks, the guardrails, the error recovery — the harness, in the original sense of the word: the tack that turns an unpredictable force into useful work.

What appreciates while the model depreciates

So let us take inventory of what remains when the model goes. First, the evaluations. “evals are surprisingly often all you need,” wrote Greg Brockman, OpenAI's president, as early as 2023[11]; the practitioner Drew Breunig distilled it into the formula I have made my own: your eval is the most valuable AI asset you own — not your model, not your prompts[12]. An evaluation suite that encodes what your business considers a job well done makes you master of your own timeline: every new model becomes a candidate you can audition in a few hours, and a forced migration, a formality. Hamel Husain, whose work is the reference on the subject, observes that failing AI products almost all share the same root cause — the absence of a robust evaluation system[13]. And Berkeley's research on “criteria drift” adds the decisive argument: evaluation criteria do not pre-exist, they emerge from use — which makes evals an asset forged by each organization, impossible to buy off the shelf and therefore durably differentiating[14].

Next come context and memory. Andrej Karpathy popularized the term “context engineering” — “the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step”[15] — and Anthropic has turned it into an engineering discipline in its own right, in which context is treated as “a finite resource with diminishing marginal returns”[16]. And what does that context contain, if not what the enterprise knows: its business rules, its conventions, its living documentation, the history of its decisions? This organizational memory, structured to be served to agents at the right moment, holds its value across every model present and future. Finally, the integration layer itself has standardized: the Model Context Protocol, launched by Anthropic in late 2024, since adopted by OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft, then entrusted to a neutral foundation under the Linux Foundation, with more than ten thousand servers published[17] — while the LLMOps tooling market grows at more than 20% a year[18]. The pipes, too, outlive the models that flow through them.

The agentic factory manifesto

From all this I draw four rules, which I apply in the projects I advise and which serve as the manifesto for what we call an agentic factory. One: never marry a model — demand of every architecture that it pass the “replacement test,” meaning the model can be swapped in days, not quarters, with evaluation evidence to prove it. Two: budget evals as infrastructure, not as project debt — it is the only AI investment whose value grows mechanically over time, since every failure case encountered in production enriches it. Three: treat memory and context as intellectual property — what your agents know about your company is yours, versioned, audited, portable from one model to the next. Four: industrialize the loop, not the prompt — guardrails, cross-checks, and error recovery are what turn an impressive demo into a system you dare to depend on.

The title of this paper is self-evident to anyone who has lived through a forced migration, but it carries a budgetary consequence few organizations have drawn: if the model is a consumable and the harness an asset, then the allocation of investment must invert. Less energy chasing the latest benchmark, more energy building what makes any benchmark exploitable. Models will come and go — that is their job, and so much the better, since each generation is better than the last. Your job is not to guess which one will win. It is to own, the day it arrives, the one thing it cannot bring you: the machine that turns it into value.
