The token paradox

August 2026 · 11 min read · Anthony Damotte

Token prices are falling tenfold every year, and yet enterprise AI bills have never climbed faster. This is no accounting anomaly: it is Jevons paradox at work, and it changes the very nature of the AI budget — which stops being an overhead line item and becomes an architecture parameter.

Discuss on LinkedIn

At my first agentic AI hackathon, I burned through five hundred euros' worth of tokens in a single afternoon — half the budget set aside for eighty participants. At the time, the figure drew smiles, and it still comes up in conversation now and then. But with hindsight, the anecdote tells a more interesting story than mere engineering gluttony: it foreshadowed, in miniature, the economic transformation every finance department is discovering today. An agent that actually works consumes on a scale that bears no comparison with a human having a conversation. And that is precisely why it pays off.

Deflation without industrial precedent

Let us begin with the cheerful side of the paradox. At equal performance, the price of inference is collapsing at a pace no other technology has ever matched — not even the semiconductor in the glory years of Moore's law. Guido Appenzeller, a partner at Andreessen Horowitz, was the first to document it under the name “LLMflation”: for equivalent model quality, the cost drops tenfold every year[1]. Getting GPT-3-level quality cost sixty dollars per million tokens in late 2021; three years later, six cents was enough — a thousand times less.

That figure is not the extrapolation of an over-enthusiastic venture capitalist. Epoch AI, the independent reference on AI's quantitative trajectories, measures annual deflation ranging from 9x to 900x depending on the task — and 40x per year to reach GPT-4 level on PhD-level science questions[2]. In late 2025, an MIT FutureTech team led by Neil Thompson confirmed the order of magnitude academically: roughly 10x per year at constant performance on frontier models, with an essential share coming from algorithmic efficiency, not just hardware[3]. The raw material of artificial intelligence is in free-fall deflation.

And yet, the bills keep exploding

If the unit price is divided by ten every year, budgets should be melting away. The opposite is happening, and with remarkable violence. Menlo Ventures measures enterprise AI spending multiplied by 3.2 in a single year — from $11.5 to $37 billion between 2024 and 2025, and by twenty-two since 2023[4]. Gartner puts worldwide GenAI spending at $644 billion for 2025, up 76%, and forecasts another +117% on models in 2026[5]. In a16z's survey of one hundred CIOs, one of them sums up the situation in a sentence every finance department should meditate on: “what I spent in 2023 I now spend in a week”[6].

A nineteenth-century economist had already understood this mechanism completely. William Stanley Jevons observed in 1865 that the more coal-efficient the steam engine became, the more coal England burned: efficiency does not reduce the consumption of a resource, it multiplies it by opening up uses that were previously out of reach. When DeepSeek sent markets into a panic in January 2025 by proving that models could be trained and served for a fraction of the accepted cost, Satya Nadella replied with a single sentence: “Jevons paradox strikes again! As AI gets more efficient and accessible, we will see its use skyrocket”[7].

It took thirteen months for the prophecy to be verified in measurable form. On OpenRouter, the largest independent multi-model aggregator, tokens consumed by agentic systems overtook those consumed by humans around February 1, 2026 — a historic crossover established on 450 trillion tokens of real traffic[8]. Machines now read and write more than we do. Deflation did not shrink the bill; it made the agent economically viable, and the agent blew up the volume.

The agent is a token factory

To understand why, you have to look at what an agent actually does with its context. Anthropic's engineering team, describing its own multi-agent research system, gives the orders of magnitude: an agent consumes roughly four times more tokens than a conversation, and a multi-agent system roughly fifteen times more[9]. On software development tasks, Erik Brynjolfsson's team at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab measures a ratio that can reach one thousand to one compared with a simple coding chat, dominated by input tokens: at every iteration of its loop, the agent re-reads its entire context, which snowballs[10].

The same study reveals a more unsettling fact: on an identical task, the execution cost of the same agent varies by a factor of thirty from one attempt to the next, and agents are incapable of predicting their own consumption. That should give pause to anyone signing off on a budget: we have industrialized a workforce whose wages are unpredictable to within 3,000%. But there is a counterpart, and it is decisive: at Anthropic, token volume alone explains 80% of the variance in system performance[9]. Tokens are not a cost leaking away; they are literally the material the quality of the result is made of.

Budget becomes an architecture parameter

This is where the paradox stops being an economic curiosity and becomes an engineer's business. If token volume determines performance, then deciding how much an agentic system is allowed to consume, and where, amounts to drawing its architecture. How many adversarial checks per deliverable? How deep should the loop run before escalating to a human? Which model for which tier — an expensive frontier model for the final arbitration, a compact model for bulk triage? Should the whole context be re-read, or is it worth investing in a memory that summarizes it? Each of these questions is simultaneously a question of quality and a question of cost. Prompt caching illustrates the shift well: properly architected, re-reading the context costs ten times less[11] — but that is a design decision, not a procurement negotiation.

Organizations have caught on at spectacular speed. The FinOps Foundation, which standardizes cloud cost management for large IT departments, measured 31% of teams managing AI spend in 2024, 63% in 2025 — and 98% in 2026, with dedicated indicators such as cost per inference[12]. At Gartner, the July 2026 analysis notes that AI budgets are coming “under increased scrutiny, with an emphasis on usage efficiency, cost control and measurable outcomes”[13]. And yet, in the largest survey of teams running agents in production, cost ranks only behind quality on the list of blockers[14] — proof that practitioners have already internalized the lesson: you don't cut tokens, you place them.

What to take away

Three convictions to close. First: stop budgeting AI like a software license and budget it like production capacity — the right indicator is not the monthly bill, it is the cost per verified outcome, and you must accept that more ambitious outcomes mean higher consumption. Second: demand that the token budget appear in your teams' architecture documents, on the same footing as latency or availability — an agentic system that cannot say where its tokens go is a system that cannot be steered. Third: beware of easy savings, because deflation is working for you — what you refuse to consume today will cost ten times less next year, but the competitors who learned a year earlier how to place their tokens will not be caught up at a discount.

As for my five hundred hackathon euros: set against what they taught me about harness design — lessons I have been applying to production systems ever since — they are probably the cheapest training I have ever received. The token paradox, in the end, fits in one sentence: yesterday's waste becomes tomorrow's under-investment, and the only real mistake is to treat as an accountant what has become an architect's decision.
