White paper

Models come and go, the harness remains

August 2026 · 10 min read · Anthony Damotte

A frontier model now lives twelve to eighteen months before it is retired from the API that serves it. Building your AI strategy on a model is therefore building on sand — and yet that is exactly what most failing projects do. What accumulates and appreciates while models depreciate goes by other names: evals, harnesses, memory, data. That is the entire reason an agentic factory exists.

Discuss on LinkedIn
Key takeaways
  • The effective lifespan of a frontier model in the API is 12 to 18 months (GPT-4.5: less than 5 months) — forced migration is structural, documented by the providers themselves.
  • The massive failures of AI pilots (~95% with no P&L impact per MIT, >40% of agentic projects headed for cancellation per Gartner) are organizational, not technological: what is missing is the factory around the model.
  • The capability-reliability gap does not close by waiting for the next model: two years of capability gains produced only modest reliability gains (Princeton). What closes it: the harness.
  • The durable assets — evals, context/memory, standardized integration, guardrails — appreciate over time. “Your eval is the most valuable AI asset you own.”
01 · The wrong question

“Which model should we choose?”

In every technology leadership conversation there is one question I am invariably asked, often within the first few minutes: “which model should we choose?” I understand the question — it resembles the structuring choices of an earlier era: the database, the language, the cloud. But it rests on a false premise. An AI model is not a foundation: it is a high-performance consumable, with a lifespan measured in months. The real question — the one that separates the 5% of projects that create value from the rest — is: what are you building around it that will outlive the model?

Model churn is not an impression: it is data anyone can verify on the providers' official deprecation pages[1][2].

The contractual notice periods give the measure of the phenomenon: sixty days of guaranteed minimum at Anthropic, six months for stabilized models at OpenAI. In late 2025, Anthropic even published formal commitments on preserving model weights, acknowledging in black and white what every production team knows: forced migrations are structural, costly, and here to stay[3]. This is the actual regime we build in — a central component of our systems gets replaced, willingly or not, on an annual cadence.

03 · The misunderstanding

Rereading the “95% failure rate”

It is against this backdrop that the most talked-about statistic of 2025 deserves a second reading: according to MIT's NANDA project, roughly 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact[4]. The figure made the front page of Fortune and briefly rattled the markets[5].

An honest reading of the number

Critics rightly pointed out that any pilot not deployed at scale is counted as a failure, and that the report documents less a failure of the technology than an organizational inability to evaluate, buy, and integrate[6]. It is precisely this corrected reading that is interesting — and corroborated on every side.

04 · The gap

Capability and reliability do not progress together

Why does the factory matter more than the machine? Because a model's raw capability and a system's reliability are two distinct quantities:

  • τ-bench (Sierra), now a standard for customer-service agents: the best agents completed fewer than one task in two — and their success rate collapsed to ~25% when eight consecutive successes at the same task were required (pass^8)[9]. An agent that succeeds once does not succeed eight times in a row.
  • Princeton (Arvind Narayanan's team, ICML 2026) has just formalized this “capability-reliability gap”: across twelve reliability metrics applied to fifteen models, nearly two years of spectacular capability progress produced only modest reliability gains[10].

Waiting for the next model will not close the gap. What closes it: the rules, the checks, the guardrails, the error recovery — the harness, in the original sense of the word: the tack that turns an unpredictable force into useful work.

05 · The durable assets

What appreciates while the model depreciates

Evaluations — the number-one asset

“Your eval is the most valuable AI asset you own — not your model, not your prompts.” — Drew Breunig[12], in the lineage of “evals are surprisingly often all you need” by Greg Brockman, OpenAI's president[11]

An evaluation suite that encodes what your business considers a job well done makes you master of your own timeline: every new model becomes a candidate you can audition in a few hours, and a forced migration, a formality. Hamel Husain, whose work is the reference on the subject, observes that failing AI products almost all share the same root cause — the absence of a robust evaluation system[13]. And Berkeley's research on “criteria drift” adds the decisive argument: evaluation criteria do not pre-exist, they emerge from use — which makes evals an asset forged by each organization, impossible to buy off the shelf and therefore durably differentiating[14].

Context and memory

Andrej Karpathy popularized “context engineering” — “the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step”[15] — and Anthropic has turned it into an engineering discipline in its own right, in which context is treated as “a finite resource with diminishing marginal returns”[16]. And what does that context contain, if not what the enterprise knows — its business rules, its conventions, its living documentation, the history of its decisions? This organizational memory, structured to be served to agents at the right moment, holds its value across every model present and future.

Integration, now standardized

The Model Context Protocol, launched by Anthropic in late 2024, adopted by OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft, has been entrusted to a neutral foundation under the Linux Foundation, with more than ten thousand servers published[17] — while the LLMOps tooling market grows at more than 20% a year[18]. The pipes, too, outlive the models that flow through them.

06 · The manifesto

Four agentic factory rules

Never marry a model.

Demand of every architecture that it pass the “replacement test”: swap models in days, not quarters, with evaluation evidence to prove it.

Budget evals as infrastructure.

Not as project debt. It is the only AI investment whose value grows mechanically over time, since every failure case encountered in production enriches it.

Treat memory and context as intellectual property.

What your agents know about your company is yours: versioned, audited, portable from one model to the next.

Industrialize the loop, not the prompt.

Guardrails, cross-checks, and error recovery: what turns an impressive demo into a system you dare to depend on.

This title is self-evident to anyone who has lived through a forced migration, but it carries a budgetary consequence few organizations have drawn: if the model is a consumable and the harness an asset, then the allocation of investment must invert. Less energy chasing the latest benchmark, more energy building what makes any benchmark exploitable. Models will come and go — that is their job, and so much the better. Your job is not to guess which one will win: it is to own, the day it arrives, the one thing it cannot bring you — the machine that turns it into value.
