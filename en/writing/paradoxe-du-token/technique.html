The token paradox

August 2026, updated September 2026 · 16 min read · Anthony Damotte

At equal performance, the cost of an answer falls roughly thirteenfold every year, and yet worldwide AI spending still jumps by nearly 50% in 2026. Jevons paradox explains the gap: efficiency multiplies uses, and agents have become the hungriest of them. For an IT department, the AI budget then leaves the overhead line and becomes an architecture parameter.

Discuss on LinkedIn
In brief
  • At equal performance, the cost of an answer falls by a factor of thirteen per year according to Epoch AI, and by five to ten according to MIT FutureTech[1][2].
  • This deflation has blind spots: companies buy the best model available, whose running price rises three- to eighteenfold per year, and the way tokens are counted has changed[2][5][6].
  • Budgets rise anyway: Gartner forecasts $2.7 trillion of worldwide AI spending in 2026, up 49.5%[7]. On OpenRouter, agents have consumed more tokens than humans since February 1, 2026[11].
  • An agent re-reads its whole context at every step, and its consumption varies by up to a factor of thirty from one run to the next on the same task[14]. Deciding where tokens are spent is therefore an architecture question.
01 · The starting point

Five hundred euros of experimentation

In one of my first agentic AI experiments, an afternoon spent running agents in a loop on real code, I consumed five hundred euros' worth of tokens. The figure made me wince at first. With hindsight, that afternoon foreshadowed on a small scale what finance departments are now seeing on their bills: an agent at work consumes on a scale that bears no comparison with what a human consumes while chatting, and that work is exactly what is being bought.

02 · Price at equal performance

At equal performance, the price of inference is collapsing

The paradox involves two opposing movements. The first is the fall in the price of inference, meaning the cost for a model of producing an answer. That price is counted in tokens, the fragments of words into which the model cuts the text it reads and writes, and which providers bill per million. At a given level of performance, it falls very fast.

The price of the most recent capabilities is therefore the one that falls fastest. Summer 2026 even prompted talk of a price war:

  • at the end of July, OpenAI cut the price of GPT-5.6 Luna, the cheapest in its range, by 80%, and that of GPT-5.6 Terra, its mid-range model, by 20%[4];
  • in August, Anthropic dropped the increase it had announced for Sonnet 5, which stayed at $2 per million input tokens[4][5].
03 · The blind spots

A deflation with blind spots

These measurements hold performance fixed. Companies, however, want the best model available, and that tier costs more.

3 to 18× / yr
rise in the running price of frontier models, driven by larger models and longer reasoning (MIT FutureTech)[2]
$10 vs $4
price per million input tokens at Anthropic, Fable 5.1 versus Opus 5.5[5]
+30%
more tokens produced by the tokenizer since Claude 4.7 for the same text; OpenRouter measured an effective cost increase of 12 to 27% on real requests, excluding very short ones[5][6]
What these figures change when reading a price list

The tokenizer is the tool that cuts text into tokens. When it produces more of them for the same text, the price displayed per million tokens is a poor guide to the price of a task.

04 · Bills keep rising

And yet, bills keep rising

The second movement concerns budgets. If the cost at equal performance falls five- to thirteenfold every year, budgets should shrink. They do the opposite.

Jevons, in 1865 and in 2025

The economist William Stanley Jevons had described this mechanism as early as 1865. As the steam engine became more economical with coal, England burned more of it, because the lower cost of use opened up applications that had previously been too expensive. In January 2025, when DeepSeek shook the markets by claiming to have trained a high-performing model for a fraction of the accepted cost, Satya Nadella, Microsoft's chief executive, invoked the same paradox to predict a surge in usage[10]; twenty months later, the 2026 figures prove him right.

05 · Agents on OpenRouter

On OpenRouter, agents now consume more than humans

The rise in volumes has an identifiable engine: agents. OpenRouter is an aggregator that gives access to the models of many providers.

Falling prices made agents affordable, and their consumption grows much faster than prices fall, even though caching absorbs a large share of it.

06 · The agent re-reads everything

The agent re-reads everything, at every step

An agent consumes this much because it re-reads its context at every iteration: the open files, the results of its tools, the session history. That context grows as the task progresses, and each turn re-reads all of it.

Usage modeConsumptionSource

Development task, compared with a conversational exchange about codeon the order of a thousand times, on averageApril 2026 study, University of Michigan and Stanford, including Erik Brynjolfsson[14]

The same study reports three findings that bear directly on budgets.

  • On an identical task, the total number of tokens consumed by the same agent can vary by up to a factor of thirty from one run to the next, with the most expensive run usually costing twice the cheapest.
  • Models predict their own consumption poorly, and systematically underestimate it.
  • Accuracy often peaks at an intermediate cost level, beyond which additional tokens no longer improve it.

An employee whose cost for the same task could double, or even be multiplied by thirty, without any way of foreseeing it, would quickly worry the finance department.

Scope of the study

The study covers only eight models and a single code-repair benchmark (SWE-bench Verified), but it matches what teams observe: some of the tokens improve the result, others add nothing, and it is up to the system's architecture to tell them apart.

07 · Budget as architecture parameter

The budget becomes an architecture parameter

If token volume weighs on both quality and cost, deciding how much an agentic system may consume, and where, amounts to designing its architecture. Four parameters have to be set, and each one moves quality and the bill at the same time.

  • The number of adversarial checks per deliverable, meaning checks entrusted to a second agent whose task is to challenge the first one's work.
  • The number of iterations after which the agent hands over to a human.
  • The model assigned to each tier of the system.
  • The share of the context re-read in full or summarized in a memory.

The five families of levers according to BCG

  • Give up unnecessary calls.
  • Route each request to the appropriate model.
  • Cache.
  • Cap consumption.
  • Train staff in a relevant use of AI[15].

The gains from caching depend on how the system is designed, over which the procurement department has no hold. Cost management took up the subject within two years: the State of FinOps survey is published by the FinOps Foundation, which brings together cloud cost management practitioners. In July 2026, Gartner observed that enterprise AI budgets are under closer scrutiny, focused on efficiency of use, cost control and measurable results[18].

08 · My harness

What I apply in my own harness

For several months I have been designing an agent harness for pre-sales, that is, the set of rules, tools and specialized agents that frame Claude Code's work on commercial proposals, including costings and presentations. The rules that weighed most on the bill concern what the agent reads.

Each deliverable has its own session.

The context does not carry the exchanges of another topic.

Bulky reading is entrusted to a secondary agent.

Whether it is a client reference document or web monitoring, only the summary comes back into the main session.

Files of more than twenty thousand characters are produced by script.

The model writes the script once and then runs it, which avoids writing the whole file into the session context.

The visual check of a presentation covers a sample of pages.

Every page inspected as an image enters the model's context; a sample is enough to catch layout defects, which repeat from one page to the next.

The choice of model follows the same concern: concentrate spending where an error would cost the most. The review agents work with a high reasoning effort (the setting that determines how many tokens the model devotes to thinking before it answers), and only the adversarial reviewer, whose role is to challenge every figure by cross-checking it against its source before a deliverable is handed over, uses the maximum setting.

≈ $13
average cost per developer per day of active Claude Code use, across Anthropic's enterprise deployments[13]
< $30

These amounts remain modest: on average, a day of Claude Code use costs less than an hour of a consultant's time, and the bill depends mainly on where the tokens are placed.

09 · Takeaways

Three convictions

I draw three convictions from this for the departments that budget for agentic AI.

The AI budget is steered like a production capacity.

The useful indicator is the cost per verified outcome, which relates consumption to a deliverable whose quality has been checked. When the ambition of the result rises, consumption rises with it, and the monthly bill alone does not tell whether the increase is justified.

The token budget belongs in architecture documents.

It appears there on the same footing as latency or availability. A team that cannot say where its system's tokens go cannot steer it, nor rely on the agent, which the study cited above shows to be a poor predictor of its own consumption.

Postponing a use to save money is paid for in learning delay.

Falling prices make waiting tempting: a capability set aside today will cost about ten times less in a year. The competitors who have meanwhile learned how to place their tokens will nonetheless keep their lead, because that know-how is acquired through practice.

As for my five hundred euros of experimentation, set against what they taught me about harness design, they remain the cheapest training I have ever taken. A spending cap set that day by the accounting department would have deprived me of that learning for five hundred euros of savings.
